Gregory describes himself as a “tinker.” Give him something new to learn, a machine to figure out, or a problem to solve, and he will find a way to understand it. “I got into everything,” he says. That curiosity has shaped a lifetime of work and learning.

Gregory has built an impressive range of technical skills over the years. He worked in construction, eventually becoming a foreman and regional foreman, and learned concrete, framing, ironwork, heavy machinery, electrical work, flooring, and tile. He became a machinist, worked in digital printing, helped an architect interpret and draw plans, and eventually ran his own landscaping business. He describes himself simply as “pretty work-oriented,” and his history makes that impossible to dispute.

But Gregory’s abilities aren’t limited to technical work. He is an artist, too. Boxes of doodles through the years inspired a friend to buy him a tattoo set. Gregory eventually apprenticed, became licensed, and opened his own tattoo shop. He had a special talent working with color, carefully choosing and mixing different shades to bring portraits and animals to life. “I can color really good, you know,” he says, before launching into an explanation of the various greens and blues needed for a frog.

Gregory lights up when he talks about his seven kids and their accomplishments. His children have inherited his talents and curiosities in their own ways. His son, Greg Jr., has a talent for computers and once took apart his dad’s computer simply to understand how it worked, and with hesitant watchful eyes then successfully put it back together. Four of Gregory’s five sons are in college, while his two daughters are studying at Oxford.

For someone with Gregory’s experience and work ethic, finding employment should be an opportunity, not an obstacle. But seeing beyond a background check can turn an employer from an interview to an interrogation. Gregory knows he has something to offer. He has spent decades learning trades, running businesses, solving problems, and working hard. Yet even with all those skills, getting someone to give him a chance can be the hardest part.

> “I don’t have a job. I need a job. I need to do something.”

That is what made this program meaningful to Gregory. When he first joined, his motivation was simple: “I don’t have a job. I need a job. I need to do something.” More than anything, he wanted to feel useful again. “I want to feel accomplished,” he explained. The program gave him a way to put his abilities to use and have something meaningful to accomplish each day.

Gregory’s story is a reminder that a person’s circumstances do not erase their abilities. His creativity, technical knowledge, work ethic, and determination were there long before he entered the program. Sometimes what a person needs most is not another lesson in what they lack, but someone willing to recognize what they already have.

Gregory has spent a lifetime building things. With the right opportunity, he can keep building and accomplish his goals.
