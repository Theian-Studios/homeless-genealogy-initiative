When Candace first joined STEP, she was looking for work.

She found much more than that.

The project gives her meaningful employment during a difficult season, but it also gives her a community and a chance to help others apart from her normal volunteering. Before long, people naturally began turning to Candace with questions. She wrote down everything so she wouldn’t forget it, then started writing up instructions to help new participants who felt overwhelmed by the project. “They can do it,” she says. Sometimes they just need someone to help them believe *they* can. If someone needs help, Candace will pull up a chair. If someone is standing alone, Candace invites them into the conversation because, as she says, “I don’t like people feeling left out.”

Quickly, she became someone others naturally turned to. Candace humbly jokes she feels as if she’s become the project’s “street mom.” She laughs at the nickname, but it reflects who she has always been.

Candace is the mother of three children. When her nephew needed help, she adopted him, too. Caring for people isn’t something she decides to do. It’s simply how she moves through the world. “Everyone needs somebody who cares,” she says. “I try to be kind to everybody.” That kindness has become part of the culture of the project.

“I’ve seen people who always looked sad and discouraged start smiling more,” Candace says. “They’re standing a little straighter.” The change she’s seen in others through the program mirrors what’s happened in her own life. “I think everything comes back to hope.”

> “Everyone needs somebody who cares. I try to be kind to everybody.”

She remembers a season when hope felt almost impossible. “There was a point where I felt like I couldn’t keep up anymore,” she says. “I felt like I was drowning, and I was ready to just let everything happen.” Today, her outlook is different. “Now I feel like there’s at least a little hope.” The genealogy project didn’t solve every challenge overnight, but it became a turning point.

“Since I started doing this project, other opportunities have started coming my way,” she says. “I wish they’d come earlier, but I’ll gladly take them now.” That hope isn’t blind optimism. It’s something Candace has seen before. “My dad got out of homelessness,” she says. “I believe in myself. I don’t know why I can’t put it all together, but I do believe in myself, and I know I can do it. I’m a hard worker, and I have integrity.”

The project has also provided something tangible during an uncertain season. The income has helped Candace keep a small storage unit filled with the belongings that tell the story of her family. Among the boxes are bags holding her mother’s hoodies. She keeps them sealed so they’ll still carry her mom’s scent, a link to something that feels familiar and hopeful. For Candace, they’re a reminder that the people we love are always more than what’s left behind. They’re also a reminder that every person is more than what can be understood at first glance.

Perhaps that’s why the word *homeless* has never sat well with Candace. “I’d rather have a term that reflects someone who’s trying,” she says. “Something that captures the effort people are making instead of only their circumstances… Once you’re labeled, people stop seeing you as an individual,” she says. One word can’t capture a mother raising four children. It can’t capture someone who quietly welcomes newcomers, patiently teaches others, or notices when someone is sitting alone. It can’t capture the person encouraging others until they’re smiling more and standing a little straighter.

STEP has given Candace meaningful work, renewed confidence, and, as she says, “at least a little hope.” For her, it’s important not only to keep going, but to lend a hand along the way, smile a little more, and lift up others as they rise together.
